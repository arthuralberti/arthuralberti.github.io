Bio

I am an economist based in São Paulo, Brazil, and hold a Master’s degree in Economic Theory from the University of São Paulo. I also received my bachelor’s degree in economics from Insper and studied as a visiting undergraduate student at the University of California, Berkeley.

My research interests lie at the intersection of political economy, public policy, and development economics, with a focus on how local institutions and political incentives shape policy implementation. I also work as a Research Assistant for Claudio Ferraz and Caroline Le Pennec on Promises, Policies, and Accountability.

Working Papers

Who Benefits from Benefits? Municipal Consequences of Federal Cash Transfers

Presented at: Brazilian Econometric Society (2026); LACEA 2026, University of São Paulo

Stage: under revision for first submission

Media Coverage: The Electoral Bet on Bolsa Família May Not Pay Off (Folha de S.Paulo, 2026)

Abstract (–): Centralized welfare programs limit local discretion over eligibility and payments, yet access often depends on municipal bureaucracies. We study whether local politicians obtain electoral returns from a federally designed cash transfer when their remaining discretion lies in registration. In Brazil’s Bolsa Família, a 2009 reform unexpectedly changed the formula assigning municipal beneficiary quotas, and a later federal revision opened additional slots during the same mayoral term. We measure the cumulative authorization change relative to the allocation implied by the previous rule. In the 2,078 municipalities where the mayor elected in 2008 sought reelection in 2012, greater federal authorization led to more new family registrations and higher realized beneficiary enrollment. The 2SLS estimate implies that 100 additional beneficiary families per 10,000 residents raise the incumbent’s vote share by 1.70 percentage points. Enrollment expanded both with and without a pre-existing local welfare center (CRAS), but we find a clear electoral response only in CRAS municipalities. Within that group, the vote-share response is larger where offices report active outreach and more intensive service to Bolsa Família families. These patterns are consistent with electoral returns arising where municipal welfare offices bring potential beneficiaries into contact with the local administration. Federal control over eligibility and payment does not eliminate local political returns when program access still depends on municipal action.

Media as Political Currency

with Alexsandros Cavgias and Luís Meloni

Presented at: Econometric Society European Winter Meeting (2026); Brazilian Econometric Society (2025); LACEA (2025)

Stage: under revision for first submission

Abstract (–): Executives who allocate broadcast licenses hold a resource fiscal transfers cannot match: it keeps working after it is granted. In Brazil’s 1987–88 National Constituent Assembly the president licensed radio and television at will while Congress wrote a new Constitution. We link the administrative record of radio and television grants to every 1986 federal-deputy candidate. In a close-election regression discontinuity design, winning a seat raised concession receipt by 14.0 percentage points, and 18.8 points among the governing coalition, and none at twelve placebo cutoffs. Recipients align with the government 36 percentage points more on institutional votes than on ideological ones, a gap measured within deputy. The incumbency premium is 26.2 percentage points larger for media-owning legislators than for non-owners. A probabilistic-voting model of executive-legislative bargaining predicts all three patterns: broadcast concessions are productive political capital, paying their electoral return in combination with office.
